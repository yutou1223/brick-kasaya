Solitary Journey Across the Desert The Moon over Chang'an · Xuanzang and the 800-li Moheyan Desert

More than thirteen centuries ago, a young man walked alone into a dead land that even the birds dared not cross.

Xuanzang Moheyan Desert Desert Yellow Dunhuang Inner Light

On an early morning in the third year of the Zhenguan era, the morning bell of Chang'an had just fallen silent and the camel bells of the Sogdian traders in the Western Market still hung in the air. Outside the city gate stood the master Xuanzang, looking back one last time at the splendid capital.

His pack was simple: a brocade kasaya, a nine-ring staff, and a heart firmer than gold. Behind him lay the glory of an age in which envoys from every nation came to court, and the fine horses and carriages granted by the emperor — all of which he politely declined. For he knew that true practice is not performed in grand halls, but in the yellow sand beneath one's feet.

“Master, ahead lies the 800-li Moheyan Desert — a dead land even the birds dare not fly across!”

the soldier at the gate could not help warning him.

Xuanzang only smiled a little, smoothed the kasaya the wind had tangled, and fixed his eyes on the red sun rising in the distance. “I would rather go west and die,” he said, “than turn east and live.”

And so he took his first step.

The moment the artwork captures is exactly this: the instant he walks into the great desert. That streak of bright yellow is the “imperial yellow” common in Tang-dynasty murals, a colour of the sacred and the luminous, and a token of the faith burning in him like the midday sun. His figure looks small and endearing, and yet he grips the staff so tightly, and sets down every step so steadily.

This was not merely a journey. It was a pilgrimage of courage.

Today we have printed that small figure on a phone case. When you feel worn down by this fast modern city and want to give up, look down at him — the Tang monk who, more than a thousand years ago, walked alone into the vast desert.

He is telling you: even when the road ahead holds nothing but sand and sun, while there is light within, there is a road beneath your feet.

Culture Notes

The little monk on this phone case is the real master Xuanzang.
The 800-li Moheyan Desert is the gobi between present-day Hami and Turpan in Xinjiang. The histories record that there “no bird flies above, no beast walks below, and neither water nor grass is found.” Xuanzang spilled his water bag in the desert and went four days and five nights without a drop, until both he and his horse collapsed on the sand. He survived because the horse, by instinct, found water.
The bright yellow on the case takes its inspiration from the Dunhuang murals. In the Tang dynasty this colour stood for light and the sacred. Xuanzang had no imperial horses or carriages; he left Chang'an with one man, one horse and one staff, slipping quietly into a column of refugees. He carried no travel permit — he was, in the truest sense, a fugitive across the border.
He travelled for seventeen years and fifty thousand li, brought back six hundred and fifty-seven Buddhist scriptures, and translated seventy-five of them — one thousand three hundred and thirty-five fascicles. The Heart Sutra he translated is still one of the Buddhist texts most familiar to Chinese readers.

So when you feel you cannot hold on any longer, look at this little monk — he crossed 800 li of dead land. What is the obstacle in front of you, compared with that?
